# Boomerification — Investment Thesis

*Framing v2, 2026-08-06. No data pulled yet; figures marked ⟨verify⟩ are from memory and must be sourced before they go in the dashboard.*

---

## 0. The problem with the first version

"The wealthiest US consumers are retiring" is true, important, and the single
most-forecasted fact in finance. It has been known with near-perfect accuracy
since the 1960s. A fact that every allocator has had four decades to price is
not, by itself, an edge.

So the thesis cannot be the demography. It has to be a claim about **which
consequences of the demography are mispriced, and why the market is structurally
slow to price them.** Everything below is organized around that.

---

## 1. Core thesis (restated)

The US is entering the payout phase of a wealth-and-age concentration that took
sixty years to build. The cohort holding the majority of household net worth is
converting from **earners and accumulators** into **spenders, decumulators, and
bequeathers** — and doing so on a predictable schedule.

The mispricing is not in *whether* this happens. It is in three gaps:

| Gap | Consensus view | Thesis view |
|---|---|---|
| **Composition, not direction** | Retirees sell assets → market supply shock | They mostly *die holding*. The real effect is a shift in demand **composition** — income and capital preservation over growth — plus a multi-decade transfer. Relative value, not a directional call. |
| **Sequence, not level** | "Aging" is one trade | It is a relay race. Each ~7-year age band has a different beneficiary set. The handoff from *experiences* to *care* is happening roughly now. |
| **Inflationary, not deflationary** | Aging is disinflationary (the Japan analogy) | The US channel runs through **labor scarcity in high-skill services**, which is inflationary in exactly the categories that are hardest to substitute. This is the highest-conviction, least-consensus limb. |

**Codename caveat.** "Boomerification" is the internal name, but the mechanism
is not boomer-specific. Gen X begins turning 65 in 2030. This is a regime with a
pipeline behind it, not a one-cohort event. Frame the dashboard so it survives
the cohort label.

## 2. Where the cohort actually is (the clock)

This is the spine of the thesis. Everything is timed off it.

- Leading edge (born 1946): **80 in 2026**
- Peak birth year (1957): **69 in 2026**
- Median boomer (born ~1955): **71 in 2026**
- Trailing edge (born 1964): **62 in 2026** — still working, still accumulating
- Gen X leading edge (born 1965): **65 in 2030**

Spending by age band is well-documented and non-linear:

- **"Go-go," ~65–75** — travel, leisure, second homes, discretionary. Peak
  experience spend. *The center of mass is leaving this band now.*
- **"Slow-go," ~75–85** — spending falls, mix rotates hard to healthcare and
  home services. Housing decisions begin.
- **"No-go," ~85+** — spending concentrates almost entirely in medical and
  long-term care; asset release is forced by care costs or death.

**The actionable timing insight:** the cohort's median is at 71 and its mass is
crossing from go-go into slow-go over roughly 2026–2035. The leisure/travel trade
has already had its run and is late-cycle. The care/health-services trade is the
one with the demographic wind still ahead of it.

## 3. The five limbs

Each limb needs a mechanism, an observable, an expression, and a falsifier.
That structure *is* the dashboard spec.

### A. Decumulation and the flow question

**Mechanism.** Retirees stop contributing and begin drawing. RMDs force
distributions. But the crude "asset meltdown" version is likely wrong: assets are
concentrated in the top decile, which spends far less than its income, and much
of what is forced out of tax-deferred accounts is reinvested in taxable accounts
rather than consumed. The wealthy do not decumulate; they transfer.

**What is actually true:** the *marginal buyer* changes character. Demand shifts
from equity beta toward income, duration-matched cash flow, principal protection,
and annuitization.

**Observables:** ICI/Fed fund-flow data by vehicle; net flows into
income/dividend/covered-call and target-date-late-vintage products; annuity sales;
401(k)-to-IRA rollover volumes; retail cash and T-bill balances by age band.

**Expression:** long income-generating and annuity/retirement-income
infrastructure; underweight products dependent on continuous contribution flows.

**Falsifier:** sustained, unoffset net equity outflow from the 65+ cohort shows up
in flow data → the benign version is wrong and the directional bear case revives.

### B. The transfer, and who actually receives it

**Mechanism.** ⟨verify: commonly cited $80–100T over ~20 years⟩. The framing
error in most versions of this trade is the recipient. Assets pass **first to the
surviving spouse** — typically a woman in her late 70s or 80s — and only later to
children who are themselves in their 50s and 60s. The "millennials inherit" story
is a 2040s story, not a 2020s one.

**Why that matters commercially:** each transfer event is a moment where the
incumbent advisor relationship breaks. Attrition at the widow step and again at
the heir step is high ⟨verify magnitude⟩. **The fee pool moves before the assets
do.** Recipient generations also prefer different intermediation — fee-based,
digital-first, indexed core, alternatives sleeve.

**Observables:** RIA/wirehouse net new assets and advisor headcount; platform net
flows; trust and estate-services volumes; average advisor age (itself a supply
problem); M&A multiples in wealth management.

**Expression:** long the platforms and RIA aggregators taking share; long trust /
estate / tax-advisory infrastructure; cautious on legacy full-service models with
old client books and old advisors.

**Falsifier:** retention data shows incumbents holding heir relationships → the
share-shift is not happening and this is just a beta-on-AUM trade.

### C. Labor supply — the inflation limb

**Mechanism.** The exiting cohort is the most experienced and highest-paid, and
in specific fields it is not replaceable on any short horizon: skilled trades,
healthcare (nursing, anesthesiology, primary care), aviation, industrial and
utility engineering, ag and logistics operators. This is **not** economy-wide
labor scarcity — it is acute in non-tradable, non-automatable services.

**Consequence:** a structurally higher floor under services wage inflation, a
higher neutral rate than the 2010s, and — critically — **duration that hedges
equity risk less reliably than it did in 2010–2020.** If this limb is right, it
changes portfolio construction, not just sector selection.

**Observables:** participation rate by age band (65+ especially); job openings and
quits in health care and construction trades; apprenticeship and nursing-school
completions; wage growth in non-tradable services vs. goods; net immigration.

**Expression:** structural short duration relative to a 2010s baseline; long
labor-substituting capex (automation, robotics, industrial software); long
staffing and training in the constrained verticals; skepticism toward a
mechanical 60/40 revival.

**Falsifier — and it's a big one:** a sustained immigration surge, or 65+
participation continuing to rise (people simply working longer). Either
substantially neutralizes this limb. **Immigration policy is the single largest
swing factor in the whole thesis.**

### D. Consumption mix

**Mechanism.** Aggregate consumption does not collapse — the basket rotates.
Goods → services. Discretionary durables → healthcare, home services, and
care. This is limb C's demand-side mirror, and it follows the age-band clock in
§2 with a lag you can nearly calendar.

**Observables:** PCE by category vs. age-band population; Medicare enrollment and
utilization; home-health and hospice volumes; travel/cruise bookings by age
cohort; auto and durable-goods purchase rates by age.

**Expression:** long care delivery, home health, medical devices and consumables
with volume (not price) leverage, pharmacy and adherence infrastructure. Fade the
experience-economy trade on strength — its demographic tailwind is turning.

**Falsifier:** health utilization per capita flattens (compression of morbidity —
people staying healthier longer), which pushes the entire care curve right.

### E. Fiscal and policy — the thesis's own hedge

**Mechanism.** A rising beneficiary-to-worker ratio raises mandatory spending
against a shrinking relative tax base. OASI trust fund depletion sits in the
mid-2030s ⟨verify current CBO/Trustees date⟩. Implications: more long-end
issuance, upward pressure on term premium, and — the part that matters most here —
**political pressure on healthcare pricing.**

**This is why limb E must sit in the same dashboard as limb D.** The same
demographics that guarantee healthcare *volume* make healthcare *price* the most
politically exposed line item in the budget. Any healthcare expression should be
built on volume and cost-reduction, not on reimbursement rates holding.

**Observables:** Trustees report projections; CBO long-term outlook; Medicare
reimbursement rule changes; long-end term premium; entitlement legislation.

**Expression:** term-premium-aware duration positioning; within healthcare, prefer
volume and cost-takeout over reimbursement-rate exposure.

**Falsifier:** meaningful entitlement reform passes → repath the whole fiscal limb.

### Housing (a cross-cutting sixth, or a sub-limb of D)

Boomers hold a large share of the housing stock at legacy mortgage rates →
lock-in suppresses turnover → the "silver tsunami" of inventory is real but
arrives slowly and **geographically unevenly** (retirement metros first). Aging in
place is the dominant revealed preference, so home modification and in-home care
beat institutional volumes — even though senior housing has the better *near-term*
setup, because construction collapsed in 2020–2023 and the demand cohort arrives
regardless. Decide whether this is its own limb before building.

## 4. Why isn't this already priced?

The thesis is only investable if there is an answer here. Four candidates,
ranked by how much I trust them:

1. **Capacity lags demand.** Supply decisions made years ago (senior housing
   starts, nursing school seats, trade apprenticeships) can't flex to meet a
   demand curve that is known but arrives later. Known demand + fixed supply =
   pricing power. *Strongest mechanism.*
2. **Policy discontinuities.** Trust-fund dates and reimbursement changes are
   step functions. Markets price step functions late and abruptly.
3. **Institutional and behavioral lag.** Advisor books, product shelves, and
   distribution relationships repaper on a multi-year lag behind the client
   transitions driving them.
4. **Index composition lag.** Benchmarks reflect the earnings power of the last
   regime; passive flows follow the benchmark. *Weakest — cite only if measurable.*

**If none of these hold for a given limb, that limb is a slide, not a trade.**
Apply this test limb by limb.

## 5. The honest weaknesses

State these up front; the thesis is more credible with them than without.

- **Demographics are slow.** Correct calls can lose to carry for years. Any
  expression needs to pay to wait, or needs a discrete catalyst.
- **Limbs C and D partially contradict limbs A and E.** Labor-scarcity inflation
  and heavy long-end issuance argue for higher rates; a decumulating cohort
  bidding for income argues for demand for duration. Do not paper over this —
  **make the net rate view an explicit, dated call rather than an emergent
  property of the other limbs.**
- **Wealth concentration cuts both ways.** ⟨verify: 55+ households hold ~70%+ of
  US household net worth⟩ — but concentration *within* the cohort means the
  median retiree is under-saved. Two very different customers, two different
  trades: luxury/legacy at the top, value/safety-net at the median. The dashboard
  should probably split them.
- **Consensus risk.** Healthcare-demographics is a crowded trade. The edge, if
  any, is in sequencing and in the labor limb — not in "buy aging."

## 6. What the dashboard has to do

The framing above dictates the build:

1. **A cohort clock** — population by age band, projected forward, as the master
   time axis every other panel is indexed to.
2. **Per-limb panels** — mechanism, live observables, current read, and the
   falsifier with its tripwire condition visible.
3. **A "why not priced" scorecard** — the §4 test, scored per limb, refreshed.
4. **A contradiction monitor** — explicitly surface where limbs disagree
   (especially the rate view), rather than averaging them into mush.
5. **Positioning** — expressions mapped to limbs, sized by conviction, each
   tagged with the falsifier that would retire it.
